Formal Propositional Logic

June 26, 2026

We are all familiar with contructing arguments using natural language. This is convenient for us, as humans, as it allows us to express ourselves in the way that is most natural. However, it is not a representation that is very amenable to computation. Even in the age of large language models, computers are still not able to reason rigourously using natural language. To be truly rigourous, we have to represent our arguments in a way that is more naturally amenable to formal (ie mathematics-like) manipulations. This will require us to represent our knowledge using a mathematics-like representation, ie using symbols.

Propositions as Symbols¶

Consider the following sentence:

If I am going outside and it is not raining then I do not take a coat or an umbrella.

Note that this sentence alone is not an argument: it is a statement - a proposition, which can be be True or False. We can also see that it is a compound proposition, made up of four atomic propositions:

  • It is raining

  • I am going outside

  • I take a coat

  • I take an umbrella

These propositions are irreducible (they can’t be broken down further). We can choose to represent them using symbols. Let us choose to represent them as RRR, OOO, CCC, and UUU respectively. Using this notation we may then expect to be able to write the compound sentence is a form something like:

if (O and not(R)) then not(C or U)

This is rather cumbersome and doing the logical equivalent of algebra with our symbols will be much easier if we introduce some notation for the connectives that join our propositions together.

Connectives¶

In our example we have used four logical connectives to construct a compound statement from atomic propositions. We will also introduce a fifth connective that is widely used in logic:

The statement “I will not take a coat or an umbrella” therefore becomes ¬(C∨U)\lnot(C\lor U)¬(C∨U). Notice that in common language, we often use the word “or” to mean “exclusive-or” (XOR), which is True when one of the disjuncts is True, but not when both are True. In formal logic we will always interpret OR to mean “either or both” which is consistent with the usual interpretation of “I will not take a coat or an umbrella”: I will not take a coat, I will not take and umbrella, and I won’t take both of them.

Note that this is not the same as the if-then construct that we are familiar with in Python and other programming languages. In programming, this construction takes an action in response to some condition. In logic, material implication does not take any action, it is a binary logic operator that should be treated in the same way as conjuction and disjunction. Thus, the statement R∧O  ⟹  UR\land O \implies UR∧O⟹U is True except when R∧OR\land OR∧O is False and UUU is True.

The Truth table for Implication can seem somewhat counter intuitive. The table matches our intuition when PPP is True. When PPP is False, it reflects that we can say nothing about the conclusion. Logic does not provide us with tools that express the notion that something “might be True” so we take the possibility of something being True to be equivalent to that same thing being True.

Lets’s think about this in terms of a concrete example. Let’s define:

  • PPP: “It is raining”

  • QQQ: “I get wet”

The implication P  ⟹  QP\implies QP⟹Q translates as “If it is raining then I get wet”. If PPP and QQQ are both True, then the implication is clearly True. If PPP is True and QQQ is False (“If it is raining I don’t get wet”), then the implication is clearly False. If PPP is False and QQQ is True, (“If it is not raining I get wet”), we define the implication to be True, but this is what known as a vacuous truth: if PPP is False then nothing is implied about QQQ. A slightly clearer example of this is “If pigs could fly then hell would freeze over”: pigs cannot fly and so we can know nothing about whether hell will or won’t freeze. If you find this confusing, you are in good company: remember the Truth Table!

Let’s now revisit our sentence

If it is not raining and I am going outdoors then I will not take a coat or an umbrella.

We can rewrite this using the symbols for the atomic sentences and the logical connectives that we have just defined:

(¬R∧O)  ⟹  ¬(C∨U)(\lnot R \land O)\implies \lnot(C\lor U)(¬R∧O)⟹¬(C∨U)

We see that the implication is False when it is not raining, I am outside, and I have taken either a coat or an umbrella. This makes sense because these situation directly contradict the statement. In all other cases, the implication is True. It does not contradict the implication to not take a coat or umbrella when it is raining and I am outside, and it does not contradict the assertion to take a coat or umbrella when it is not raining and I am not outside.
